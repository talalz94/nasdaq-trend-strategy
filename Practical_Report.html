PRACTICAL·Q&A Nikkei 1990 · S&P 1927 · costs · automation
Five practical questions · three of them testable

What if the Nasdaq dies?
Japan already ran that experiment.

Your questions had real answers hiding in data I hadn't used yet. The Nikkei crashed in 1990 and fell for 23 straight years — the exact scenario you're worried about. And the S&P 500 goes back to 1927, covering the Depression, WWII and 1970s stagflation. I ran the strategy through both. I also costed every fee and tested whether re-optimising annually helps. Two answers surprised me.

01

If the index crashes and never recovers

The Nikkei peaked at 38,900 in 1989 and was at 10,400 twenty-three years later — a −5.6%/yr bear market lasting a generation. This is the strongest available proxy for "the Nasdaq tops out and dies."

EraLeverageStrategy CAGRStrategy maxDDIndex buy&holdLeveraged buy&hold
The reassuring answer

Through the 23-year collapse the strategy made +3.6%/yr at 1× while the index lost 5.6%/yr and 3× buy-and-hold lost 34.6%/yr — a total wipeout. The trend gate simply keeps you in bonds/cash for most of a bear market. You don't get rich, but you don't get destroyed.

Two costs you must accept

Leverage stops helping. In the crash era 1× beat 3× (3.6% vs 2.8%) with a −17% drawdown instead of −46%. Leverage only pays in trends.

You lag the recovery. Japan 2013–26 rose 14.4%/yr; the strategy captured only 4.8% at 1×. After a long bear the trend signal re-engages slowly. That is the price of the protection.

02

Validation before 1985 — 99 years of S&P 500

Yes, there is data, and it goes back much further than I was using: S&P 500 from 1927 (and Nasdaq Composite from 1971). This is a completely different monetary and market era — the toughest out-of-sample test available.

EraLevStrategy CAGRmaxDDCalmarBuy&hold CAGRBuy&hold maxDD
It holds up — across the Depression and stagflation

Over the full 99 years the strategy at 1× returned 6.1%/yr with a −25% drawdown vs buy-and-hold's 6.3% with −86%. Same return, one-third the pain — Calmar 0.24 vs 0.07.

Its best relative showing was the era most like today's worries: 1966–82 stagflation, where it made 7.3%/yr vs the index's 2.5%, with a −11% drawdown vs −48%. Through 1927–54 (crash, Depression, WWII) it made 4.7% vs 2.7%, again with a quarter of the drawdown.

And it confirms the leverage warning, emphatically

On the S&P across 99 years, 1× beats 3× on Calmar in every single era — 0.19 vs 0.18 in the Depression, 0.64 vs 0.18 in stagflation, 0.95 vs 0.57 in the great bull, 0.24 vs 0.17 overall. Combined with the cross-market result, the evidence is now unambiguous: 3× leverage was a Nasdaq-specific gift, not a principle.

It also lags strong bull markets — 1983–99 it made 10.4% vs the index's 14.8%, and 2000–26 it made 3.0% vs 6.3%. This is insurance, and insurance costs upside.

03

Should you re-optimise after a year?

Directly tested: re-fit the parameters every January on trailing data and use them for that year, versus never touching them. All four modes scored on the same 1998–2026 window.

ApproachCAGRMax DDCalmarSharpeParameter changes
Answer: don't re-optimise. Freeze the rules.

Frozen rules scored Calmar 0.82. Re-optimising annually on all history scored 0.69; on a 10-year lookback 0.55; on 5 years 0.55. The shorter the lookback, the worse it gets — and the more it churns (20–24 parameter changes in 29 years).

Re-optimisation chases noise. Recent data is a small sample, so the "best" parameters keep moving and you end up permanently fitted to the last cycle. The parameter plateau I found earlier (thresholds 0.20–0.30 all work) exists precisely because the exact values don't matter — so re-tuning them adds variance, not skill.

What to do instead: leave the rules alone and re-examine only if the strategy breaks in a way the backtest never showed — e.g. a drawdown materially worse than −35%, or 3+ years of underperforming buy-and-hold with no bear market to explain it. That's a structural-failure check, not an optimisation.

04

Fees, spreads and the tax problem

Annual turnover is 11.9× — meaningful but not extreme for a weekly system. Each drag added cumulatively:

Cost layerCAGRMax DDCalmarIncremental drag
Trading costs are manageable

Commissions: ~$0 at Alpaca/IBKR/Schwab for ETFs. Expense ratios (TQQQ 0.95% — already in the model, plus TLT 0.15% and GLD 0.40%) cost 0.16%/yr. Even pessimistic 20 bps spreads only take the total drag to 2.7%/yr, leaving Calmar at 0.67. The strategy is not fee-fragile.

Taxes are the real cost — and they're huge

Weekly rebalancing realises short-term gains, taxed as ordinary income. At a 35% rate a ~24% gross return nets roughly 16% — a bigger hit than every trading cost combined, several times over.

This strategy belongs in a tax-advantaged account (IRA/401k or your jurisdiction's equivalent). In a taxable account, much of the edge goes to the tax authority.

05

Can it run itself on a VM?

Yes — the strategy is unusually well suited to automation: it trades once a week, uses three liquid ETFs, and needs no intraday data. Here's what the full stack looks like.

WEEKLY CRON — Friday 15:45 ET

  1. FETCH     yfinance or Alpaca → ^NDX, ^TNX, ^IRX          (free)
  2. COMPUTE   EMA-100 · realized vol · efficiency ratio ·
                curve slope · month → target weights        (scanner.py — already built)
  3. RECONCILE read current positions via Alpaca API,
                compute the delta vs target
  4. ORDER     submit market-on-close / limit orders for the delta
                skip any trade below a ~1% drift threshold   (cuts churn & cost)
  5. LOG + ALERT append state to CSV, email/Telegram the summary

INFRASTRUCTURE
  · any $5/month VM, or a free GitHub Actions scheduled workflow
  · Alpaca API — commission-free ETFs, paper & live endpoints, keys already set up
  · idempotent: re-running the same week changes nothing
  · your only manual job: deposit cash occasionally. It self-allocates on the
    next rebalance because everything is expressed as target percentages.
What automation can't fix

The failure modes are operational, not analytical: a stale data feed, an API outage on rebalance day, a broker margin change, an ETF being delisted or restructured, or a silent bug that quietly stops rebalancing. Any of those can do far more damage than a suboptimal threshold.

So: run it in paper mode for several months first, add a dead-man's-switch alert if a weekly run doesn't report, and keep a monthly human eyeball on it. Automation removes the emotional risk of not following the rules — which is real and valuable — but it introduces engineering risk in exchange.

06

Net effect on what to expect

  • Downside protection is now evidence-backed, not hoped-for. Through a 23-year collapse (Nikkei) and the Great Depression and 1970s stagflation, the strategy stayed positive and cut drawdowns to roughly a third of buy-and-hold. That is the strongest thing this research has established.
  • Use 1× or 2×, not 3×. Two independent tests — eight markets and 99 years of S&P — both say lower leverage is better risk-adjusted. 3× only won on the Nasdaq, in one era.
  • Freeze the parameters. Re-optimising annually made things measurably worse in every variant tested.
  • Put it in a tax-advantaged account, or accept losing roughly a third of the return to short-term taxes.
  • Automate it, but paper-trade first and monitor for operational failure rather than for optimisation opportunities.
  • Realistic expectation: mid-teens percent annually in a favourable regime, mid-single-digits in a hostile one, with −25% to −35% drawdowns at 1–2× leverage. The 24% headline needed the best index in its best era.
