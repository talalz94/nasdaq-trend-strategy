ADDENDUM·VALIDATION Tier 1 tests · 8 markets
The last four tests · and the finding that reframes everything

The rules generalize.
The spectacular numbers don't.

I ran the three untested ideas and the cross-market check. Result: nothing beat the existing strategy's 0.79 Calmar — but two variants tied it with meaningfully different risk profiles, one fixed the 2022 failure for free, and the ensemble idea failed. Then the important one: applying the identical rules to eight different markets shows they beat buy-and-hold in 7 of 8 — genuine, general edge — but the median market scores 0.23 Calmar, not 0.79. The Nasdaq result is a 3× outlier.

Tier 1 — the three untested ideas

2× INSTEAD OF 3× — a tie, not an upgrade
  • 2× at vol-target 0.30: Calmar 0.79 (identical), Sharpe 0.97 (better), drawdown −25% vs −31%.
  • But CAGR falls 24.2% → 19.5%. It's a different point on the same risk curve, not free improvement.
  • Also softened 2011: −16.2% vs −21.6%.
DURATION-MATCHED BONDS — free fix for 2022
  • Short bills when rates are rising, long bonds when falling.
  • 2022: −21.9% → −14.1% at identical 0.79 Calmar.
  • Catch: it gives back deflationary-crisis protection (dot-com +4.4%→−2.3%, GFC −0.3%→−5.2%). A regime swap, not a pure win.
SIGNAL ENSEMBLE — failed
  • Averaging 4 EMA lookbacks into continuous exposure: Calmar 0.69–0.72 vs 0.79.
  • It did soften 2011 (−18.2%) — but diluted the signal everywhere else, which cost more than the whipsaw it saved.
  • My hypothesis was wrong; the single clean trend gate is better.
So there is no single "best" — there's a menu

Three configurations tie at 0.79 Calmar. Which you'd pick depends entirely on what you care about, not on which is "better".

The good news — the rules are real

The strategy beat 1× buy-and-hold on Calmar in 7 of 8 markets and 3× buy-and-hold in 8 of 8. Median Calmar 0.23 vs 0.12 for buy-and-hold — the rules roughly double risk-adjusted return across unrelated markets. That is not what a curve-fit looks like.

The sobering news — Nasdaq is an outlier

Nasdaq scores 0.73. The next best is Canada at 0.33; the median market is 0.23; the S&P only 0.16. The strategy needs a strongly and persistently trending market, and the Nasdaq trended harder than anything else in this era. Gold miners actually lost (−0.01).

03

Which components generalize — and which was luck

Same perturbations, but now measured as the median across all eight markets. This separates real structure from Nasdaq-specific fortune.

The single most important line in this whole project

Across eight markets, less leverage is better risk-adjusted: 1× scores a median Calmar of 0.29, 2× scores 0.26, 3× scores 0.23. The Nasdaq is the only market where 3× was the right answer.

That means the leverage choice — the most exciting part of the strategy — is the part least supported by evidence. It worked on the Nasdaq because the Nasdaq's trend was strong enough to outrun the decay. There is no guarantee that persists.

What the cross-market test confirms

Both of the rules I derived from the Nasdaq hold up independently elsewhere: removing the half-size-in-chop rule drops the median from 0.23 → 0.16, and removing the Aug–Sep rule drops it to 0.20. Both earn their place on out-of-market evidence, which is much stronger than the in-sample case.

04

Revised expectations

  • The strategy design is sound. The rules add value in unrelated markets, each component is independently justified, and it beats buy-and-hold nearly everywhere. That conclusion survived every test I could devise.
  • The 24%/yr headline should not be your planning number. It assumes the Nasdaq keeps out-trending every other major market as it did for 40 years. If the next decades look like the median market instead, the same rules deliver roughly 9–14%/yr with −40% to −55% drawdowns.
  • Consider 2× rather than 3×. It ties on Calmar, wins on Sharpe, cuts the drawdown by 6 points, and — critically — is the choice better supported by cross-market evidence.
  • Take the duration-matched bond leg if you think inflation/rate shocks are the bigger forward risk; keep long bonds if you think deflationary crashes are. It's a genuine either/or.
  • Two things remain unfixed: V-shaped whipsaws (the ensemble idea failed to solve it) and the stocks-and-bonds-down regime (duration-matching helps but doesn't cure).
Is this the best that can be done?

On this data, close to it. I've now tested ~230 variants; the measured selection cost (0.07–0.15 Calmar) means further searching is roughly as likely to mislead as to help. The remaining honest gains are not in the backtest — they're in implementation: taxes and account type, using futures instead of leveraged ETFs to avoid decay and fees, position sizing relative to total net worth, and paper-trading long enough to know whether you can actually hold through a −30% drawdown.

05

Caveats specific to this addendum

  • The 2× sleeve is simulated (validated against real QLD: correlation 0.996, 4.3%/yr tracking error, conservative — sim x70 vs real x84 over 20 years).
  • Cross-market leveraged sleeves are all simulated from index returns with the same 0.95% expense + financing model. Real 3× products for DAX/Nikkei/HSI either don't exist or are illiquid — so those rows are a test of the rules, not investable strategies.
  • All markets share one off-leg (US 10-year treasuries), which flatters non-US markets slightly.
  • Eight markets is a small sample and they are correlated. 7-of-8 is encouraging, not conclusive.

Method — 2× sleeve: 2×index − 0.95%/252 − 1×T-bill, daily-compounded, validated vs QLD 2006–2026. Ensemble: mean of price>EMA(50/100/150/200) as continuous exposure. Duration-matched off-leg: synthetic 2-year (duration 1.9) when 10-y yield > its 200-day MA, else synthetic 10-year (duration 7.5). Cross-market: ^NDX, ^GSPC, ^RUT, ^GDAXI, ^N225, ^HSI, ^GSPTSE, ^XAU with per-market signals, weekly rebalance, 3 bps/turnover, no lookahead. Code: tier1.py, crossmarket.py, engine.py.
