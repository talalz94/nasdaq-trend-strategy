Phase C · why the chart goes flat, and what fixes it

Two flat stretches,
two different diseases.

You spotted flat spots around 1992–95 and 2000–2010. They have opposite causes. In 2000–2010 the Nasdaq itself lost 4.6%/yr — a Nasdaq-or-cash strategy simply had nothing to harvest. In 1992–95 the Nasdaq rose +14.8%/yr and the strategy still took a −39% drawdown — that one was self-inflicted whipsaw. The first needs other assets; the second was already fixed in Phase B. The final answer lifts the dead decade from 6.2% → 11.0%/yr and cuts the worst drawdown to −33%.

01

Research first — was there anything to make?

Before building anything, I checked what actually trended during each flat window, using assets with history back to the mid-80s (gold miners ^XAU from 1983, synthetic 10-y Treasuries from ^TNX, validated at 0.95 correlation to IEF). The answer is emphatic: 2000–2010 was only flat if you were in Nasdaq.

Diagnosis

2000–2010: gold miners +11.6%/yr, bonds +6.4%, Canada +4.4%, small caps +4.1% — real trends existed, just not in tech. A structural diversification problem.

1992–1995: Nasdaq did +14.8%/yr. The opportunity was there and the strategy still bled. That's the whipsaw disease — and Phase B's half-leverage fix already addressed it.

02

The obvious fix failed

First attempt: full multi-asset momentum rotation — rank everything by 12-month momentum, hold the trending winners. It made things worse.

ApproachCAGRMax DDCalmar2000–10
Why it backfired

The rotation pool was full of equity-like alternatives (small caps, Hong Kong, international) that fall with Nasdaq in a crisis — so it added risk without adding diversification. And momentum-chasing gold miners means buying an asset with its own −83% drawdown after it has already run.

Rotating the defensive leg by momentum ("best of bonds/gold") was even worse — 2.4% in 2000–10 vs 7.9% just holding cash. Timing the diversifiers destroys their value.

03

What worked: static, boring diversification

Two small changes, both economically motivated, neither involving any timing:

1 · BONDS AS THE OFF-LEG
  • When the Nasdaq sleeve is risk-off, hold 10-y Treasuries instead of cash.
  • Strictly better: CAGR 24.5→26.1%, same −39% drawdown, and the 2000s decade jumps 6.2% → 9.2%.
  • Works because treasuries rally in the exact flight-to-safety moments that trigger risk-off.
2 · A SMALL PERMANENT GOLD SLEEVE
  • Hold 15% in gold at all times, alongside the engine.
  • 2000s decade 9.2% → 11.0%, drawdown −39% → −33%, Calmar 0.63 → 0.72.
  • Sizing is not a knife-edge: anything from 10–25% gives Calmar 0.71–0.72.
GOLD-SLEEVE SIZE SWEEP — A BROAD PLATEAU, NOT A LUCKY POINT
Be honest about what the gold sleeve is

It is insurance, not alpha. It improved exactly one decade — the 2000s (+4.8pp) — and cost a little in every other one (1990s 42.9→38.4%, 2010s 22.9→21.2%, 2020s 32.0→29.8%). Its Calmar gain comes mostly from cutting drawdown, not adding return.

So the real question isn't "does it backtest well" — it's do you want to pay ~1–4%/yr in normal decades to be protected in a stagflationary one? That's a judgement call, not a data one.

06

Caveats & what's next

  • The gold sleeve is not walk-forward validated — it helped one decade out of five. Treat it as a deliberate insurance premium, not a proven edge.
  • Selection cost still applies (~0.15 Calmar from Phase B). Discount these in-sample figures accordingly.
  • Pre-2010 TQQQ is simulated; gold is proxied by the miners index (^XAU) pre-2004, which is higher-beta than bullion. Real bullion would likely be gentler and slightly less effective.
  • 1988–2026 contained the greatest tech bull in history. Don't extrapolate 24%/yr.
