We win the head-to-head.
Then one test undercuts the win.

I searched the literature and the communities, then re-implemented every credible strategy on our own data, period and cost model — because published CAGRs from different eras and assets aren't comparable. Our strategy ranked #1 and #2 of 11. But a fairness check exposed the catch: on the S&P 500, a far simpler published rule beats ours. Our edge is real, and it is Nasdaq-specific.

Notable: our core idea is not original — and that's reassuring

Gayed's "Leverage for the Long Run" won the 2016 Charles H. Dow Award for essentially our core concept: leverage when above the 200-day MA, T-bills below. Independent academic validation of the foundation we arrived at empirically is a good sign — it means the base isn't a fluke of our dataset.

Also striking: several unrelated strategies cluster at Calmar ≈ 0.78–0.80 (Keller's BAA at 0.78 published, the vol-target-12% approach at ~0.78, ours at 0.79). That looks like a practical ceiling for this family rather than a coincidence.

What our extra machinery buys

The cleanest comparison is Gayed's rule applied to the Nasdaq (row 5): same asset, same 3× leverage, just simpler rules. It earns 22.7% CAGR — close to our 24.1% — but with an −82% drawdown versus our −33%. HFEA, confirmed fragile

The famous Bogleheads portfolio finishes near the bottom: 13.9% CAGR with a −70% drawdown, and −63% in 2022 alone. It relies on stocks and bonds being negatively correlated — which stopped being true precisely when it mattered. Our research reached the same conclusion independently.

03

The fairness test — is it the rules or the asset?

Our strategy was built on Nasdaq data; the others were built elsewhere and are being tested out-of-sample here. That's a large home-field advantage. So I ran our ruleset and Gayed's on the same asset, same rebalance, same off-leg, same costs — on both indices.

ON THE NASDAQ — ours wins decisively
At 3×: our Calmar 0.68 vs Gayed's 0.30. At 1×: 0.79 vs 0.37. More than double, on the asset we actually intend to trade.
ON THE S&P 500 — the simpler rule wins
At 3×: Gayed 0.27 vs ours 0.22. At 1×: 0.34 vs 0.27. Our extra machinery actively hurts here.

The honest interpretation

Our parameters — 20% vol target, 0.25 efficiency threshold, EMA-100 — were tuned to the Nasdaq's volatility signature. The S&P is calmer, so those settings misfire, while Gayed's rule has no parameters to misfit: it's just above/below a line.

So the head-to-head victory is genuine but conditional. On the Nasdaq — the thing we built for and validated across 40 years, 8 markets, the Nikkei collapse and 99 years of S&P — ours is clearly the better tool. As a universal improvement over published methods, it is not. If you ever apply this to a different index, start from the simple 200-day rule, not from our parameters.

04

Did I find anything better?

  • No — nothing beat our strategy on the Nasdaq, which is what we're building for. Of 11 strategies re-tested on identical terms, ours took the top two spots on Calmar.
  • But nothing found was junk, either. Keller's BAA (published Calmar ~0.78) is genuinely competitive risk-adjusted — it just targets ~11% return with a −14% drawdown instead of ~20% with −25%. That's a different objective, not an inferior strategy. If you want a smoother ride, that family deserves a real look.
  • Our core idea has independent academic validation — Gayed's Dow Award paper is the same foundation. What we added on top (vol targeting, partial de-levering, defensive sleeves) is where our advantage lives.
  • The 2022 problem is universal. Every leveraged strategy tested was hurt: HFEA −63%, Gayed −53%, vol-target −24%, ours −25%. Nobody in the literature has solved stocks-and-bonds-falling-together either.
  • What I could not test: managed-futures funds (short free history), Medallion-style stat-arb (not replicable), options-overlay strategies, and anything requiring paid data. Managed futures remain the most credible untested candidate for the 2022 regime.
The meta-point worth keeping

Multiple independent researchers, using different methods across different decades, keep landing near Calmar 0.8. We landed there too. That convergence suggests we're near a real ceiling for trend-following on a single equity index — and that anyone claiming dramatically better is probably overfitting, selling something, or measuring a favourable era.

05

Caveats on this comparison

  • My re-implementations of the multi-asset TAA strategies are approximations. VAA-G4, GTAA and GEM needed asset substitutions (no REITs or EM ETFs before ~2003, so I used Hang Seng and gold as proxies) and simplified rules. My VAA scored 0.31 against BAA's published 0.78 — the gap is likely my implementation, not the strategies. Treat those rows as indicative, not definitive.
  • Home-field advantage is large and unquantified. Ours is in-sample here; theirs is out-of-sample.
  • Leveraged sleeves are simulated (UPRO/TMF/TQQQ pre-inception) with a conservative expense + financing model.
  • Published figures differ from my re-runs for legitimate reasons — different eras, assets, rebalance rules and cost assumptions. I report both so the difference is visible rather than hidden.

Method — every strategy re-coded on the same 1988–2026 daily panel, weekly/monthly/quarterly rebalance per each strategy's own specification, signals from prior close (no lookahead), 3 bps per unit turnover, identical statistics. Simulated sleeves: UPRO 3×S&P, TMF 3×20y treasuries (duration 17), TQQQ/QLD 3×/2× Nasdaq — each net of expense ratio and financing. Code: external.py, fairtest.py, engine.py.
